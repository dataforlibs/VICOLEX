Russia & Poland in Ukraine's History

How often the Library of Congress DK508 (Ukraine — History) catalog's own subject headings associate Ukraine's history with Russia, Poland, and other neighboring or imperial powers, year by year from 650 BC to 2020 AD. Dashed lines mark well-documented political turning points — hover one, or anywhere on the chart, for detail.

What this measures: for each year, how many subject-heading date-subdivisions in the catalog — whose place is the actor's own name ("Russia," "Poland," "Soviet Union," never a shared/Ukrainian place like "Galicia" or "Crimea") — have a date range covering that year, summed across all 7,093 records. Concretely: a single heading dated, say, "1917-1991" is counted once for every one of those 75 years, not once for the book — so it adds the same total weight as 75 separate single-year headings would, just spread across the chart instead of piled on one year. That's deliberate: it's what lets one long-spanning survey book show up as sustained coverage across the whole period it discusses, and it's why the numbers on this chart are called mention-years, not mentions or books — the same book is "counted" many times if its subject heading spans many years. This is documented association in the bibliography, not adjudicated territorial control or political legitimacy. Ukraine's own subject heading (— mention-years across this span — several times Russia, Poland, and Other combined) is drawn as a thin reference line, not a stacked band, so it gives scale without burying the Russia/Poland story the filled areas exist to tell. No subject heading in this catalog names Russia, Poland, or these other powers before 1501, though Ukraine's own line runs continuously back to 650 BC (ancient Black Sea colonies). The x-axis is also non-linear, the same compression used by the companion map's year sliders: years before 1900 are squeezed to keep the chart focused on the 20th–21st century, where 85% of the Russia/Poland/Other data actually is — nothing is dropped, just given less width (see the dashed marker on the chart, or the table view for exact by-decade values either side of it).

Every book behind the areas/line above, layered on top in that same band's own color, one mark per about-mention. Why a dot and not a line, or vice versa, comes straight from how the catalog itself dated that mention: a dot means the subject heading names one specific year (e.g. "…—1991—"), a line means it names an explicit span (e.g. "…—1917-1921—" or "To 1648") — the mark just draws whatever precision the cataloger originally recorded, nothing is estimated or rounded here. Both are scattered at a fixed random height inside their band purely to keep marks from stacking directly on top of each other — vertical position within a band carries no meaning, and a wide, imprecise range is drawn fainter than a short, precise one so century-spanning surveys don't visually bury specific mentions. Within the Russia band only, marks split into three shades by language: warm orange is Ukrainian, plain red is Russian specifically, and a third, dimmer red is Polish/English/German/anything else — kept separate from plain red so "red" always means Russian, not "everyone who isn't Ukrainian." (Poland and Other stay a single color — the same shading looks muddy on blue or green, so it isn't used there.) Ukraine's own gold marks are every one of its 10,670 mentions — it alone has ~8x as many as the other three bands combined, so expect a much denser cluster of gold than any other color here; that density is real, not a rendering artifact.
Reading the shape: Ukraine's own line dwarfs the other three throughout — even at their closest, in 1991, Russia + Poland + Other combined (615 mention-years) still comes to under 40% of Ukraine's own total that year (1,606). Before 1900 the gap is starker still: no subject heading names Russia, Poland, or these other powers before 1501, and combined they stay in the single digits through the entire 19th century, while Ukraine's own heading — thin as it is that far back — already runs continuously to 650 BC. That earliest stretch comes from a real, specific place: records about Olbia, an ancient Greek colony on the Black Sea coast that the Library of Congress catalogs under the subject heading "Olbia (Extinct city)" — literally tagged extinct, since it's an archaeological site today, not a living settlement — with a documented span of roughly 650 BC to 350 AD, sitting on the territory of modern Ukraine. It's why the line starts exactly where it does: not an estimate or a rounding artifact, but the founding-to-abandonment date range this catalog's own cataloging assigns to that one ancient city.

The Russia band's real growth starts in the interwar period: it averages about 16 mention-years a year across 1901–1920, then jumps past 220 through the 1920s–30s and stays roughly there — a long plateau, not a steady climb — for the rest of the Soviet era (average 231 across 1946–1985) and into the 1990s–2000s, peaking at 596 in 1991 alone, its single highest point in the whole span. Poland, by contrast, never leaves a narrow band in the teens-to-30s across the entire 20th century — present continuously, but never approaching Russia's scale. Other powers barely register at any point, rarely reaching double digits.

That timing isn't incidental. Poland's numbers step from 14–15 to 33 right at 1918 — the Ukrainian–Polish War — and hold in the 25–37 range through 1939, precisely the years Poland administered Galicia and Volhynia. Russia's do the same at the 1917 revolution (14 to 25), then jump again through 1939–1945 and hold near 230 for the rest of the Soviet era — years Ukraine was administratively part of the Russian/Soviet state. The underlying subject headings are broader than "occupation" alone (diplomatic relations, military history, boundary disputes, biographies, and each power's own internal political history cataloged alongside a Ukraine-focused record all count), so this is closer to "how strongly the catalog's own vocabulary associates each period with these two neighbors" than a direct occupation-year tally — but the alignment with real political control in the 20th century is real, not noise. It's much weaker earlier: the pre-1900 numbers are thin enough (often single digits) that they don't show the same clean step at 1569 (Union of Lublin) or 1772 (Partitions of Poland) that the 20th century shows at 1917–1918.

Ukraine's own line spikes the same year Russia's does: both series hit their all-time peak in 1991, the year of independence — then Ukraine's line stays elevated through the mid-2000s (averaging 1,224 mention-years/year across 1991–2005) before falling off substantially. Every series drops sharply after 2014, which is worth reading carefully: a subject heading can only cover a year once some cataloged record's date range reaches that far, and the data behind this chart doesn't extend past 2020 — so the drop-off in the most recent years is very likely a data-coverage edge effect (too little time for coverage to accumulate), not evidence that interest in this history actually declined after 2014.

Zero-value stretches on this chart are doing real analytical work, but they're not all the same kind of "gap." The Russia, Poland, and Other bands share one enormous structural gap — every single year before 1501 (2,201 consecutive years) — which is a hard fact about this catalog's holdings: no subject heading here names any of those powers that early, full stop. A subtler kind shows up inside the Other band specifically, which goes quiet twice in the 18th–19th century — 1743 to 1799 (57 years) and 1801 to 1866 (66 years), broken only by a single active year, 1800 — and that one is genuinely ambiguous: it could mean this catalog holds nothing that documents another power's association with Ukraine across those decades, or it could mean cataloging practice for that era simply didn't apply this kind of dated subject heading as often, independent of what actually happened historically. Telling those two apart isn't something the chart can do on its own — it's exactly the kind of question a zero stretch like this is meant to prompt a closer look at, rather than settle by itself. The 2015–2020 drop-off above is the third kind, an edge effect of the data's own end date rather than a gap in the history it describes.
